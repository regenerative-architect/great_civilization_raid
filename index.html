A civic participation manual · offline-first · zero tracking

Humanity is on a raid. The enemies are failure modes — not people.

This is a field guide for the unglamorous, cooperative work of keeping shared systems alive: food, water, health, housing, trust, ecosystems, and the institutions that hold them together. It borrows the shape of a cooperative game because that shape is useful — clear roles, shared quests, honest difficulty ratings — not because the stakes are fictional.

"Humanity is participating in a cooperative civilization-scale raid in which the enemies are failure modes — not peoples, nations, ideologies, classes, cultures, religions, or individuals."

This is the one sentence the whole manual is built to protect. If any tool below ever seems to point at a person or a group instead of a pattern, that tool has failed its brief — see the Safety & Zero-Harm Charter.

01 · Onboarding

Start Here in Five Minutes

You do not need to read this whole manual before doing something useful. Answer seven quick prompts and land on one safe first action.

  1. Pick a domain. Skim the seventeen Domain Rooms — food, water, housing, health, information integrity, and so on — and notice which one you already think about most.
  2. Pick a scale. Household, block, neighborhood, city, region, nation, or planet — see the Scale Map. Most useful first quests live at household or block scale.
  3. Pick your likely class. Skim the thirteen Raid Classes, or take the two-minute Class Finder quiz in that section for a heuristic guess.
  4. Identify one failure mode. Skim the Boss Index for the pattern that best matches what's actually going wrong — remember, the boss is the pattern, never a person.
  5. Choose one safe quest. Open the Quest Engine, filter to your domain and a low risk level, and pick a MICRO or LOCAL tier quest you can finish this week.
  6. Record what happened. Mark the quest's status, jot a one-line note, and record whatever evidence the quest card asks for — this is what makes the next attempt (by you or anyone else) smarter.
  7. Decide the next move. Every quest card suggests a "possible next quest." Take it, sit with the result for a while, or stop — all three are legitimate.

02 · Onboarding

Where Do I Fit?

Realistic contribution pathways for common starting conditions. Nobody has unlimited time, money, or authority — these are honest starting points, not a ranking of who matters more.

"I have 10 minutes and no money."▸

Ten minutes is enough to file one accurate report of a broken thing, leave one useful comment on a public consultation, or forward one piece of verified information instead of an unverified one. Look at MICRO tier quests in the Quest Engine filtered to "Zero budget." Small, frequent, verified actions beat rare grand ones.

"I have 2 hours a week."▸

Two hours a week, kept up for a season, is enough to become the person who actually understands one recurring local problem. Pick one LOCAL or COMMUNITY tier quest and treat the two hours as a standing appointment. Consistency compounds faster than intensity.

"I am a student."▸

Students have something scarce: legitimate, unpressured time to read primary sources and ask questions adults are too busy or too embarrassed to ask. RESEARCH and KNOWLEDGE tier quests fit well around a term calendar, and the Researcher and Archivist classes are natural starting points.

"I am retired."▸

Institutional memory and unhurried attention are rare resources. The Archivist, Educator, and Steward / Maintainer classes lean on exactly this. MAINTENANCE and KNOWLEDGE tier quests especially benefit from people who remember how something used to work.

"I work inside government."▸

You have access most people never get: procedure, budget lines, and the ability to change a default. See Institutional Mode and its checklist directly. INSTITUTIONAL tier quests like "reduce form friction" or "publish decision provenance" are often achievable from inside without needing anyone's permission to start looking.

"I own a small business."▸

Procurement choices, hiring practices, and which vendors you pay on time are quiet levers with real reach. See the Lever Library entries on procurement, apprenticeship, and local economic circulation. The Logistician class maps closely onto day-to-day operations you already run.

"I am an engineer."▸

Interoperability, redundancy, and graceful degradation are design choices, not accidents. The Crafter and Systems Player classes fit naturally, and infrastructure-adjacent domains (energy, water, digital infrastructure, transport) usually have a technical quest at every tier from MICRO to REGIONAL.

"I am a teacher."▸

Teaching is how a lesson propagates past a single person's lifetime — it is the mechanism behind Principle 12 (build systems that can continue without you). The Educator class and any BRIDGE-BUILDING or KNOWLEDGE tier quest built around explaining, translating, or training are strong fits.

"I am a caregiver."▸

Caregiving is already stewardship work under a different name, and it usually leaves little slack for anything else — that is a real constraint, not a failure of effort. The Healer class describes what you are likely already doing. Look for MICRO tier, indoor, zero-budget quests that fit inside a caregiving schedule rather than around it.

"I am unemployed but want to contribute."▸

Time without income is still a real resource, and contribution here does not require a job title. RESEARCH, KNOWLEDGE, and COMMUNITY tier quests are open to anyone, cost nothing, and often produce something concrete enough to describe in a future interview — verified local information, a repaired tool library catalogue, a documented process.

"I prefer research rather than public-facing work."▸

Good. Verification work is chronically undersupplied relative to advocacy work. The Researcher and Archivist classes, and the "Verified Before Amplifying" achievement in Stewardship & Achievements, exist specifically to make this contribution visible and valued.

"I prefer hands-on work."▸

The Crafter, Regenerator, and Stabilizer classes are built around doing rather than deliberating. LOCAL and REGENERATION tier quests tend to have the shortest distance between deciding and starting.

Non-negotiable

Safety & Zero-Harm Charter

Every tool in this manual is built inside this fence. Nothing below is optional, and nothing later in the document overrides it.

No people are bosses.

The Boss Index names patterns — coordination failure, opacity, capture, burnout — never people, nations, ideologies, cultures, religions, or identities. If you ever find yourself using this manual's language to describe a specific person as an enemy, stop: that is a misuse of every tool here, not a clever application of one.

This manual will not help with

  • Coercion, vigilantism, or taking enforcement into your own hands
  • Harassment, doxxing, or targeting an identifiable individual
  • Sabotage, threats, or political violence of any kind
  • Dehumanization, discrimination, or collective punishment of a group
  • Unsafe or medical experimentation on people without oversight
  • Bypassing emergency procedures or illegal access to systems
  • Manipulation, deceptive persuasion, or engineered consent
  • Privacy violations, surveillance, or covert data collection

This manual actively encourages

  • Lawful action, taken in the open
  • Informed consent from everyone an action affects
  • Community legitimacy — invitation and mandate, not imposition
  • Expert review for anything touching health, safety, or law
  • Reversible, small-scale piloting before scaling anything
  • Transparent evidence, shared where others can check it
  • Routing genuine emergencies to appropriate emergency services first
  • Extra care for vulnerable people, including free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) for anyone doing ecological or land-based work that touches Indigenous rights, land, or knowledge

The Zero Harm & Anti-Coercion Covenant

This manual is offered as a tool for repair, never for control. It will not knowingly help plan harm to a person, a group, or a living system, and it treats consent, transparency, and reversibility as non-negotiable design constraints rather than nice-to-haves. Anyone extending this file inherits this covenant — see Attributions & Licenses.

Foundations

Claims & Evidence Discipline

Six words this manual tries to use precisely. Borrowing them for your own notes and quest write-ups is one of the highest-leverage habits in the whole document.

OBSERVATION

Something directly seen, measured, or recorded, with minimal interpretation. "The library's opening hours sign says 9am–5pm, but the door was locked at 9:20am on three separate visits."

INFERENCE

A reasonable conclusion drawn from an observation, one small step removed from the raw fact. "The posted hours are probably not being followed reliably."

HYPOTHESIS

An explanation offered for testing, not yet confirmed. "Staffing may be short on weekday mornings."

MODEL

A simplified representation built to explore a question, useful for learning even when it is not literally true. The Scenario Simulator's output is always a MODEL.

NORMATIVE CHOICE

A value judgment about what should happen, not a factual claim about what is happening. "Public services should open reliably at their posted hours" is a normative choice most people share, but it is still a choice, not a measurement.

VERIFIED RESULT

A claim checked against independent evidence strongly enough that reasonable, informed people would agree it holds. Rare, and worth flagging when you actually reach it.

Most disagreements in civic work are not disagreements about facts — they are two people at different rungs of this ladder mistaking each other for being on the same one. Naming the rung out loud ("that sounds like a hypothesis to me, not a verified result yet") de-escalates faster than arguing the content.

The Civilizational Model

Fourteen capacities, held in tension. Optimizing any single one at the expense of the rest is one of the most common ways complex systems break.

A civilization is not one number going up. It is many interacting capacities, and pushing hard on one — say, short-term economic output — while starving another — say, ecological capacity or social trust — tends to produce a system that looks strong right up until it doesn't. The fourteen capacities below are the ones this manual tracks. They are a simplification, not a periodic table: treat them as a shared vocabulary for a conversation, not a finished science.

Human capacity

Health, skills, time, and energy available across a population.

Ecological capacity

The headroom of the living systems everything else depends on.

Infrastructure capacity

Physical systems — water, power, transport, shelter — and their condition.

Knowledge capacity

What is understood, recorded, and able to be taught forward.

Institutional capacity

The ability of organizations to actually do what they exist to do.

Governance capacity

Legitimate, workable rules for making shared decisions.

Repair capacity

The ability to fix what breaks, on purpose, before it cascades.

Social trust

Confidence that others and institutions will act in good faith.

Economic resilience

The ability to absorb a shock without lasting collapse in livelihoods.

Coordination capacity

The ability of many actors to act coherently without central control.

Cultural capacity

Shared stories, norms, and meaning that make cooperation feel natural.

Technological capacity

Tools and know-how, and whether they are broadly accessible.

Local autonomy

The ability of a community to decide its own affairs at the smallest workable scale (see subsidiarity below).

Future option space

How many reasonable paths remain open for people who are not born yet.

Dynamics worth knowing by name

Bottlenecks & weakest-link dynamics

A system's overall performance is often set by its single scarcest capacity, not its average one — reinforcing everything except the bottleneck rarely helps.

Feedback loops & delays

Effects that loop back to strengthen (reinforcing) or dampen (balancing) a change — often after a delay long enough to make the original cause hard to see.

Thresholds & path dependence

Some changes are smooth; others cross a threshold into a new, hard-to-reverse regime, and which path you took starts to constrain which paths remain open.

Lock-in & cascading failure

Early choices can become so embedded that alternatives disappear (lock-in); and a failure in one tightly coupled part can spread faster than anyone can respond (cascade).

Externalities & rebound effects

Costs pushed onto people who had no say (externalities), and efficiency gains that get partly eaten by increased use (rebound) rather than banked as savings.

Second-order effects

The effect of the effect — a fix that solves the immediate problem while quietly creating the next one.

Fragility, redundancy & slack

Fragility breaks under stress that a redundant (backup-having) or slack-holding (deliberately under-optimized) system would absorb.

Modularity & interoperability

Parts that can be swapped independently (modularity) and that speak a common enough language to work together (interoperability) — both reduce the cost of fixing one thing without breaking another.

Subsidiarity & polycentric governance

Decisions made at the smallest capable scale (subsidiarity), inside many overlapping centers of authority rather than one (polycentric) — Ostrom's central finding about durable commons governance.

Graceful degradation

A well-designed system fails partially and visibly, buying time to respond, instead of failing totally and silently.

Regenerative capacity

The ability of a system to actively rebuild its own stock of capacity — ecological, social, or institutional — rather than merely avoid depleting it.

Stabilizer

Keep an already-strained system from getting worse right now.

▸
Core abilities
Triage under pressure, fast calm decisions, stopping small failures from cascading.
Real-world equivalents
Paramedics, ER staff, disaster-response volunteers, on-call engineers, the neighbor who runs the phone tree during an outage.
Beginner action
Learn your household's or block's actual emergency contacts and shutoffs.
Intermediate action
Take a basic first-aid or community emergency response (CERT-style) course.
Advanced action
Help design the response plan itself, not just execute it.
Safeguards
Hand off to Repair or Regeneration once danger passes; staying in crisis mode indefinitely burns people out.
Failure modes
Treating every problem as an emergency; becoming indispensable by design.
Complementary classes
Healer, Logistician, Steward / Maintainer.
Example quest
Map your household's and block's actual points of failure before you need to know them.
Institutional analogue
Emergency management office; incident command systems.
You may already be doing this if…
you're the person people call first when something breaks.

Healer

Tend to the human cost of a failure — before, during, and after.

▸
Core abilities
Active listening, trauma awareness, connecting people to care, sustaining morale.
Real-world equivalents
Nurses, therapists, chaplains, community health workers, mutual-aid coordinators.
Beginner action
Practice active listening without immediately jumping to problem-solving.
Intermediate action
Learn Mental Health First Aid or an equivalent local course.
Advanced action
Help design shifts, check-ins, and debriefs that prevent burnout at scale.
Safeguards
Care work has real limits; refer out to professionals for anything beyond your training.
Failure modes
Carrying an unsustainable emotional load; substituting presence for needed professional care.
Complementary classes
Stabilizer, Educator, Bridge Builder.
Example quest
Set up a standing, low-effort check-in with one person carrying a heavy load right now.
Institutional analogue
Public health and social services; employee assistance programs.
You may already be doing this if…
people come to you first when something is wrong, not just when something is broken.

Crafter

Build and repair the physical and digital things a community depends on.

▸
Core abilities
Hands-on making, repair-over-replace instincts, tool literacy.
Real-world equivalents
Electricians, plumbers, carpenters, developers maintaining shared tools, repair-café volunteers.
Beginner action
Learn to fix one thing you currently just replace.
Intermediate action
Volunteer at or start a repair café or tool library.
Advanced action
Design for repairability from the start — modularity, standard parts, open documentation.
Safeguards
Work within your competence and code/safety requirements; know when a licensed professional is required.
Failure modes
Over-engineering a fix nobody else can maintain; skipping documentation because it seems obvious.
Complementary classes
Steward / Maintainer, Systems Player, Regenerator.
Example quest
Fix one broken shared thing and leave a note explaining how, for the next person.
Institutional analogue
Public works; facilities management.
You may already be doing this if…
you're the one who fixes things instead of throwing them out.

Researcher

Find out what is actually true before anyone acts on what merely seems true.

▸
Core abilities
Source-checking, causal reasoning, comfort with uncertainty, writing findings clearly.
Real-world equivalents
Scientists, verification journalists, epidemiologists, citizen scientists.
Beginner action
Practice tracing one claim back to its original source.
Intermediate action
Run one small, structured local investigation — a count, a survey, a measurement.
Advanced action
Design studies or audits that others can replicate.
Safeguards
Publish uncertainty honestly rather than false certainty or endless delay.
Failure modes
Verification that never becomes action; unconsciously seeking confirming evidence.
Complementary classes
Archivist, Systems Player, Educator.
Example quest
Pick one widely repeated local claim and trace it back to its actual original source.
Institutional analogue
Universities; public-health surveillance; auditors-general.
You may already be doing this if…
you're the one who checks before sharing.

Scout

Notice a problem while it is still small, cheap, and easy to fix.

▸
Core abilities
Pattern recognition, early-warning sense, comfort raising an unwelcome flag early.
Real-world equivalents
Building inspectors, sentinel-surveillance workers, whistleblowers who use proper channels.
Beginner action
Start a simple personal log of small anomalies you notice.
Intermediate action
Learn the actual reporting channel for your domain — a 311 line, safety hotline, ombudsperson.
Advanced action
Help design an early-warning system others can use.
Safeguards
Report through legitimate channels; a Scout's job is to notice and flag, never to punish or expose people.
Failure modes
Crying wolf until real warnings get ignored; noticing everything, prioritizing nothing.
Complementary classes
Researcher, Archivist, Stabilizer.
Example quest
Report one small, verifiable hazard through the correct official channel and note how long it took to be addressed.
Institutional analogue
Inspectorates; sentinel surveillance networks.
You may already be doing this if…
you notice things going wrong before anyone else mentions them.

Archivist

Make sure what was learned last time is still findable this time.

▸
Core abilities
Documentation, organizing information, institutional memory.
Real-world equivalents
Librarians, records managers, historians, whoever keeps the shared drive from becoming chaos.
Beginner action
Write down one undocumented process before it lives only in someone's head.
Intermediate action
Build a simple, searchable index for something that currently has none.
Advanced action
Design retention and access systems that outlast any one person's involvement.
Safeguards
Respect privacy and consent in what you record; not everything should be archived openly.
Failure modes
Hoarding information instead of making it usable; archiving so much that nothing is findable.
Complementary classes
Researcher, Steward / Maintainer, Educator.
Example quest
Find one piece of undocumented but important local knowledge and write it down somewhere findable.
Institutional analogue
Public libraries; municipal records offices.
You may already be doing this if…
you're the one people ask "wait, why do we do it this way?"

Bridge Builder

Connect people or institutions that need each other but aren't talking.

▸
Core abilities
Mediation, translation across jargon and worldview, coalition-building, de-escalation.
Real-world equivalents
Community organizers, ombudspeople, interpreters, HR mediators.
Beginner action
Practice restating someone's position in words they'd agree with before responding.
Intermediate action
Facilitate one structured conversation between two mismatched parties.
Advanced action
Design ongoing structures — joint committees, shared glossaries — that outlast your involvement.
Safeguards
Never bridge-build through deception; consent and honesty are what make a bridge trustworthy.
Failure modes
Becoming a single point of failure between two groups who never learn to talk directly; false balance.
Complementary classes
Governance Architect, Healer, Systems Player.
Example quest
Set up one direct conversation between two people or groups who currently only hear about each other secondhand.
Institutional analogue
Ombudsperson's office; interagency liaison roles.
You may already be doing this if…
people on both sides of a disagreement still trust you.

Regenerator

Actively rebuild ecological or social capacity, not just slow its decline.

▸
Core abilities
Ecological literacy, long time-horizon thinking, patience with slow feedback loops.
Real-world equivalents
Restoration ecologists, urban foresters, regenerative farmers, watershed stewards.
Beginner action
Learn which species or systems are native and struggling locally.
Intermediate action
Join or start a restoration project with a multi-year commitment.
Advanced action
Design restoration that builds local knowledge and autonomy rather than dependency on outside experts.
Safeguards
Seek free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC) for any land-based work touching Indigenous rights or knowledge.
Failure modes
Restoring for the photo rather than the ecosystem's actual needs; treating a multi-year process as a one-day event.
Complementary classes
Crafter, Steward / Maintainer, Bridge Builder.
Example quest
Join an existing local restoration effort for one full season rather than one afternoon.
Institutional analogue
Parks and conservation agencies; watershed authorities.
You may already be doing this if…
you think in seasons and years, not just days.

Systems Player

See the leverage point others miss because they're looking at one part, not the whole.

▸
Core abilities
Systems mapping, spotting feedback loops and second-order effects, synthesizing across domains.
Real-world equivalents
Systems engineers, policy analysts, operations researchers.
Beginner action
Draw one causal loop diagram for a problem you actually care about.
Intermediate action
Identify a real bottleneck — not just the most visible symptom — in a system you're part of.
Advanced action
Design an intervention that targets the bottleneck, not the symptom.
Safeguards
A clever systems insight is still a HYPOTHESIS until tested — see Claims & Evidence Discipline.
Failure modes
Analysis paralysis; explaining lived experience back to the people living it.
Complementary classes
Researcher, Governance Architect, Bridge Builder.
Example quest
Map the actual causal chain behind one recurring local complaint, past its first, most obvious cause.
Institutional analogue
Policy analysis units; systems-engineering teams.
You may already be doing this if…
you keep noticing that today's fix is tomorrow's new problem.

Logistician

Get the right resources, people, and information to the right place at the right time.

▸
Core abilities
Coordination, scheduling, resource allocation, calm handling of moving parts.
Real-world equivalents
Supply-chain and operations managers, event coordinators, dispatchers, mutual-aid supply coordinators.
Beginner action
Coordinate one small, concrete handoff cleanly.
Intermediate action
Run logistics for a recurring community effort.
Advanced action
Design the coordination system itself so it survives your absence.
Safeguards
Don't let coordination become a hidden form of control — the goal is smooth flow, not personal indispensability.
Failure modes
Becoming the only person who knows how the schedule works; optimizing away all slack, leaving no buffer for the unexpected.
Complementary classes
Stabilizer, Steward / Maintainer, Crafter.
Example quest
Document one recurring coordination task well enough that someone else could run it without asking you anything.
Institutional analogue
Operations and logistics departments; emergency supply chains.
You may already be doing this if…
you're the one who ends up holding the schedule.

Governance Architect

Design the rules and decision structures that let a group decide things fairly and durably.

▸
Core abilities
Institutional design, understanding of legitimacy and authority, careful rule-writing.
Real-world equivalents
Policy designers, bylaw committees, co-op governance volunteers, union stewards.
Beginner action
Read the actual governing document of one group you're part of.
Intermediate action
Help draft or amend one rule using an explicit, fair process.
Advanced action
Design decision structures with built-in review, appeal, and succession from the start.
Safeguards
Rules written without the people they'll bind rarely hold; legitimacy is earned through process, not assumed from position.
Failure modes
Writing rules so rigid they can't adapt, or so vague they mean whatever the powerful decide.
Complementary classes
Bridge Builder, Systems Player, Steward / Maintainer.
Example quest
Find one rule in a group you belong to that nobody can explain, and propose a reason or a revision.
Institutional analogue
Legislative drafting offices; constitutional and bylaw committees.
You may already be doing this if…
you're the one who actually reads the bylaws.

Steward / Maintainer

Keep something that already works, working — quietly, reliably, for a long time.

▸
Core abilities
Discipline, follow-through, comfort with unglamorous repetition, noticing small decay early.
Real-world equivalents
Facilities staff, sysadmins, groundskeepers, long-serving treasurers, open-source maintainers.
Beginner action
Adopt one small recurring maintenance task nobody else wants.
Intermediate action
Set up a maintenance schedule with reminders so it doesn't depend on your memory.
Advanced action
Train a successor and document the role well enough that stewardship itself becomes transferable.
Safeguards
Maintenance deserves real resourcing — donating unpaid labor indefinitely to cover a funding gap is a patch, not a fix, and should be named as such.
Failure modes
Becoming an invisible single point of failure (see the Bus-Factor Test); silent, unacknowledged burnout.
Complementary classes
Crafter, Logistician, Archivist.
Example quest
Set up a recurring maintenance check for one thing that currently only gets attention after it breaks.
Institutional analogue
Facilities and asset management; open-source project maintainers.
You may already be doing this if…

Educator

Make a lesson propagate past the one person who first learned it.

▸
Core abilities
Explaining clearly at the right level, patience, curriculum and training design.
Real-world equivalents
Teachers, trainers, extension agents, whoever actually writes the onboarding doc.

Explain one thing you understand well to someone who doesn't, and ask what was still unclear.
Intermediate action
Build a short, reusable explainer instead of re-explaining from scratch each time.

Over-simplifying past the point of accuracy; building a personal following rather than transferable understanding.

Diagnosis

Boss Index

No people are bosses. Every entry below names a recurring failure pattern — never a person, nation, ideology, culture, religion, or identity. If you find yourself using this index to describe a specific person as "the boss," that is a misuse — see the Safety & Zero-Harm Charter.

Coordination Failure

Everyone agrees; nothing happens.

▸
Root drivers
No clear ownership; too many veto points and none with authority to act.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like apathy or bad faith — usually isn't.
Early-warning indicators
Meetings ending in "let's circle back"; the same issue raised three times running.
Better countermeasures
Name one accountable owner per task; use a shared visible tracker; set a default tie-breaker.
Relevant classes
Logistician, Governance Architect, Bridge Builder.
Example local intervention
A block appoints one rotating point-person instead of deciding by ambient consensus.
Example institutional intervention
An agency assigns a single accountable owner to a previously "shared" responsibility.

Tragedy of the Commons

A shared resource degrades faster than any one user intends.

▸
Root drivers
No monitoring or agreed rules; private incentive rewards taking more than a fair share.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like individual greed; usually a missing-institution problem (see Ostrom, Research Foundations).
Early-warning indicators
"Someone else will deal with it" becomes common; usage rising with nobody tracking it.
Better countermeasures
Clear boundaries, trusted monitoring, graduated consequences — rarely privatization or an outright ban.
Relevant classes
Governance Architect, Steward / Maintainer, Systems Player.
Example local intervention
Neighbors agree and post simple, monitored rules for a shared tool library or garden plot.
Example institutional intervention
A watershed authority sets extraction limits co-designed with users.

Corruption

Decisions track private benefit more than stated criteria.

▸
Root drivers
Weak transparency and audit; concentrated discretion with no check.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like one bad actor; usually a structural gap that will produce another one.
Early-warning indicators
Decisions consistently benefiting the same small circle regardless of who's officially deciding.
Better countermeasures
Published criteria, independent audit, rotation of discretionary roles, protected reporting channels.
Relevant classes
Researcher, Scout, Governance Architect.
Example local intervention
A community group proactively publishes its own spending and vote record.
Example institutional intervention
An agency publishes decision provenance for discretionary approvals (see Institutional Mode).

Opacity

People affected by a decision can't find out how or why it was made.

▸
Root drivers
No default-to-transparent norm; information treated as power rather than a shared resource.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like bureaucratic slowness; is often habitual or deliberate withholding.
Early-warning indicators
"That's not public" as a reflex, not a considered answer.
Better countermeasures
Default-open records with narrow, justified exceptions; plain-language summaries alongside technical ones.
Relevant classes
Archivist, Researcher, Governance Architect.
Example local intervention
A tenant association requests and publishes building maintenance records.
Example institutional intervention
An agency adopts open-by-default data publishing with a documented exception process.

Information Pollution

False or misleading claims spread faster than corrections.

▸
Root drivers
Sharing is frictionless and rewarded; verifying is slow and unrewarded.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like a pure media-literacy failure; is also an incentive-design failure.
Early-warning indicators
A claim spreading faster than anyone can check it, especially one confirming an existing grievance.
Better countermeasures
Verify-before-amplify habits (see Claims & Evidence Discipline); friction before resharing unverified claims.
Relevant classes
Researcher, Scout, Educator.
Example local intervention
A neighborhood page adopts a "verified" tag and a check-before-resharing norm.
Example institutional intervention
A public agency commits to rapid, plain-language rebuttal of fast-spreading misinformation about itself.

Polarization

Disagreement calcifies into identity; positions harden against evidence.

▸
Root drivers
Segregated information environments; incentives rewarding performed conflict over resolution.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like people simply disagreeing more; often a shrinking-shared-reality problem.
Early-warning indicators
Fewer venues where opposing views are heard directly rather than secondhand.
Better countermeasures
Structured dialogue around real shared stakes; bridge-building venues; name the shared problem before the disagreement.
Relevant classes
Bridge Builder, Healer, Governance Architect.
Example local intervention
A facilitated conversation between two groups who currently only hear about each other through rumor.
Example institutional intervention
A public body runs a citizen assembly with a genuinely mixed, representative panel.

Metric Gaming

The measured number improves while the underlying reality doesn't.

▸
Root drivers
A single metric became the target instead of a signal; it's easier to game than the goal is to achieve (Goodhart's law).
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like success; is a measurement-design failure.
Early-warning indicators
A metric improving suspiciously fast, or open talk of "how to hit the number."
Better countermeasures
Multiple, hard-to-game metrics together; qualitative spot-checks; reward the underlying behavior, not only the proxy.
Relevant classes
Researcher, Systems Player, Governance Architect.
Example local intervention
A volunteer program tracks outcomes reported by recipients, not only hours logged.
Example institutional intervention
An agency pairs a quantitative KPI with an independent qualitative audit.

Regulatory Capture

Rules meant to constrain an industry start reflecting its preferences.

▸
Root drivers
Revolving-door staffing; regulators dependent on the regulated for expertise or funding.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like technical necessity; often influence with a technical justification attached afterward.
Early-warning indicators
Rule changes consistently tracking industry requests more than public comment.
Better countermeasures
Cooling-off periods, independent technical review, published comment-to-outcome mapping.
Relevant classes
Researcher, Governance Architect, Scout.
Example local intervention
A resident group cross-references public comments against the final decision and publishes the gap.
Example institutional intervention
A regulator adopts a mandatory cooling-off period and independent review panel.

Monopoly / Lock-In

Switching away from a dominant option becomes prohibitively costly.

▸
Root drivers
Network effects, proprietary formats, high switching costs, weak interoperability requirements.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like consumer preference; often an absence of real alternatives.
Early-warning indicators
A shrinking number of viable alternatives; new entrants exiting quickly.
Better countermeasures
Open standards, mandated interoperability and data portability, support for smaller alternatives.
Relevant classes
Systems Player, Governance Architect, Crafter.
Example local intervention
A community adopts an open, exportable format for its own shared records.
Example institutional intervention
A regulator requires data portability between competing service providers.

Institutional Amnesia

The same mistake recurs because nobody remembers it happened before.

▸
Root drivers
High turnover with no handoff process; decisions made verbally and never recorded.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like new incompetence; usually a documentation gap.
Early-warning indicators
"Didn't we already try this?" with no record to check.
Better countermeasures
Mandatory after-action records; searchable archives; onboarding that includes past failures, not just current procedure.
Relevant classes
Archivist, Educator, Steward / Maintainer.
Example local intervention
A resident group keeps a shared, dated log of past requests and outcomes.
Example institutional intervention
An agency requires a written after-action review before closing any significant incident.

Maintenance Neglect

Things built well decay because upkeep is never funded like building was.

▸
Root drivers
Funding and recognition reward ribbon-cuttings over upkeep; maintenance stays invisible until it fails.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like sudden failure; is usually years of deferred, unfunded upkeep.
Early-warning indicators
A maintenance backlog that only grows; "temporary" fixes older than a year.
Better countermeasures
Dedicated maintenance budgets set at build time; public recognition for maintenance, not only new projects.
Relevant classes
Steward / Maintainer, Logistician, Governance Architect.
Example local intervention
A community group sets aside a small maintenance fund before building anything new.
Example institutional intervention
An agency creates a protected maintenance budget line (see Institutional Mode).

Short-Termism

Decisions favor near-term visible wins over larger, longer-term benefits.

▸
Root drivers
Election, funding, or reporting cycles shorter than the problem's real timescale.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like poor judgment; often a rational response to short-cycle incentives.
Early-warning indicators
Projects sized to fit a budget or election cycle rather than the problem's actual timescale.
Better countermeasures
Multi-year budgeting; explicit future-option accounting (see Future Generations); reward prevention, not just response.
Relevant classes
Governance Architect, Systems Player, Regenerator.
Example local intervention
A group commits multi-year, not single-event, funding to a restoration project.
Example institutional intervention
An agency adopts a mandatory Future Option Test for major capital decisions.

Preventable Scarcity

Shortage exists even though total available resources could meet the need.

▸
Root drivers
Distribution bottlenecks, information gaps about availability, eligibility friction.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like true scarcity; often an allocation or awareness failure.
Early-warning indicators
Waste and unmet need coexisting in the same system.
Better countermeasures
Matching systems connecting surplus to need; reduced eligibility friction; local redistribution networks.
Relevant classes
Logistician, Bridge Builder, Researcher.
Example local intervention
A neighborhood sets up a surplus-food redistribution point.
Example institutional intervention
An agency simplifies an eligibility process shown to block access more than it prevents fraud.

Cascading Infrastructure Failure

One failure rapidly triggers failures in dependent systems.

▸
Root drivers
Tight coupling between systems with no redundancy or isolation points.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like one unlucky failure; is a design absence of graceful degradation.
Early-warning indicators
Systems with no tested backup or manual fallback mode.
Better countermeasures
Redundancy at critical junctions; designed isolation points; regularly tested fallback procedures.
Relevant classes
Crafter, Systems Player, Stabilizer.
Example local intervention
A household or block tests its manual fallback for one critical utility once a year.
Example institutional intervention
A utility runs and documents a live-fire failover drill.

Ecological Overshoot

Use of a resource exceeds its regeneration rate.

▸
Root drivers
No feedback between use and regeneration rate; costs externalized onto ecosystems or future users.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks fine right up until a threshold is crossed — ecological delay hides the deficit.
Early-warning indicators
Yields or populations still adequate today but trending down across multiple seasons.
Better countermeasures
Use limits tied to measured regeneration rate; restoration investment alongside use.
Relevant classes
Regenerator, Researcher, Governance Architect.
Example local intervention
A community garden tracks soil health, not only yield, season over season.
Example institutional intervention
A fishery or forestry agency sets quotas tied to measured stock recovery, not political pressure.

Social Fragmentation

People who used to interact across difference increasingly don't.

▸
Root drivers
Loss of shared public space and institutions; sorting into more homogeneous groups by choice or cost.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like differing preferences; also an infrastructure-of-encounter problem.
Early-warning indicators
Fewer genuinely mixed public spaces; declining membership in cross-cutting local institutions.
Better countermeasures
Investment in public space, libraries, mixed-use community infrastructure; programming designed to mix, not sort.
Relevant classes
Bridge Builder, Governance Architect, Healer.
Example local intervention
A library or community center runs a program designed to mix age groups or backgrounds.
Example institutional intervention
A city funds public-space upkeep as a social-cohesion line item, not only a parks line item.

Exclusion

A group that should benefit from a system can't access it.

▸
Root drivers
Design built around an unstated "default" user; access barriers treated as edge cases.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like low demand from that group; usually a designed-out barrier.
Early-warning indicators
Usage data that doesn't reflect the population the service is meant to serve.
Better countermeasures
Design with excluded users from the start; remove named barriers one at a time and check whether usage shifts.
Relevant classes
Bridge Builder, Researcher, Healer.
Example local intervention
A program adds a translated version and a no-appointment option, then checks whether uptake changes.
Example institutional intervention
An agency runs an access audit against its actual service population, not its assumed one.

Bureaucratic Overload

The process for getting something done costs more than the thing itself.

▸
Root drivers
Rules accumulated over time to prevent past problems, never pruned; nobody owns removing steps, only adding them.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like caution or thoroughness; often just accreted friction with no active benefit.
Early-warning indicators
A form or process that takes longer to complete than the task it authorizes.
Better countermeasures
Regular pruning reviews; a named owner for reducing friction; test the process with a real user, not just an audit on paper.
Relevant classes
Systems Player, Governance Architect, Logistician.
Example local intervention
A community group rewrites its own onboarding form after timing how long it actually takes.
Example institutional intervention
An agency runs a "reduce form friction" quest (see Institutional Mode) with a measured before/after.

Perverse Incentives

People respond rationally to incentives that reward the wrong behavior.

▸
Root drivers
Incentive design copied from a different context; unexamined assumptions about what it will actually reward.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like bad actors; usually good actors responding sensibly to bad incentives — moral hazard (risk someone else absorbs) is a close cousin.
Early-warning indicators
People openly describing a workaround as "just following the rules as written."
Better countermeasures
Pilot incentive changes small and reversible; ask "what would a reasonable person do to maximize this?" before finalizing.
Relevant classes
Systems Player, Governance Architect, Researcher.
Example local intervention
A rewards program is redesigned after noticing it rewards volume over quality.
Example institutional intervention
An agency pilots a redesigned incentive on one small program before rolling it out fully.

Diffusion of Responsibility

A problem visible to many is acted on by none, each assuming someone else will.

▸
Root drivers
No named owner; large group size; ambiguity about who is actually supposed to act.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like nobody cares; usually everybody cares and nobody thinks it's specifically their job.
Early-warning indicators
A problem discussed repeatedly with no one volunteering to own it.
Better countermeasures
Name one specific owner, even temporarily; make asking directly ("can you take this?") normal.
Relevant classes
Logistician, Bridge Builder, Stabilizer.
Example local intervention
A group explicitly assigns one named person to a recurring shared chore.
Example institutional intervention
An agency clarifies which specific role owns a cross-departmental issue.

Burnout & Learned Helplessness

People who cared and tried stop trying, and call the system unchangeable.

▸
Root drivers
Sustained effort with no visible effect, support, or acknowledgment; repeated failure without repair.
Misleading surface symptom
Looks like apathy or laziness; usually the aftermath of unsupported, unsuccessful effort.
Early-warning indicators
Declining participation specifically among previously engaged people.
Better countermeasures
Smaller, winnable first quests to rebuild a sense of effect; visible acknowledgment of effort; shared load (see the Bus-Factor Test).
Relevant classes
Healer, Educator, Stabilizer.
Example local intervention
A group deliberately starts with one small, achievable win before a larger campaign.
Example institutional intervention
An agency creates a channel that visibly closes the loop on staff or public suggestions.

The Centralization–Fragmentation Trap

A system oscillates between too-centralized and too-fragmented.

▸
Root drivers
Treating centralization vs. decentralization as one dial instead of asking, layer by layer, which decisions belong where.
Misleading surface symptom
Each swing looks like fixing the last failure, when it's actually just swinging to the opposite one.
Early-warning indicators
A reform cycle re-litigating "centralize or localize" without ever landing.
Better countermeasures
Subsidiarity (decide at the smallest capable scale) plus polycentric coordination between scales — see The Civilizational Model.
Relevant classes
Governance Architect, Systems Player, Bridge Builder.
Example local intervention
A neighborhood federation lets each block set its own rules within small, negotiated shared boundaries.
Example institutional intervention
A regional body coordinates standards while leaving implementation to local units.

How this is calculated (read before trusting any number above)

Resilience = 60% the average of the eleven "positive" capacities, plus 40% of (100 − the average of inequality and system pressure), minus a shock penalty. The shock penalty is the shock severity, reduced by up to 70% based on your average of redundancy, maintenance, and adaptability — the idea that buffers absorb shocks, a real systems-dynamics concept, applied here in a deliberately simple, checkable way. The bottleneck is whichever positive capacity is currently lowest. None of this is calibrated against real-world data — it is a MODEL, in the sense defined in Claims & Evidence Discipline, built to make the shape of the trade-offs visible, not to predict an outcome.

How the label is decided

If rights risk, ecological risk, or unintended-consequence risk is rated 1 or 2, the result is never better than "Needs expert review," regardless of the average — one severe flag outweighs a good overall score. If reversibility and the affected-population score are both 1 or 2 together, the result is "High-risk," full stop. Otherwise, the average of all eleven scores decides: 4.0+ is "Safe to explore," 3.0–3.99 is "Pilot carefully," 2.0–2.99 is "Needs expert review," and below 2.0 is "High-risk."

Flow Map

The path a need travels before it becomes an outcome — and loops back as feedback. Most bosses strike at one specific step.

Need→ Signal→ Institution→ Decision→ Resources→ Implementation→ Outcome→ Feedback ↺

Need → Signal

Fails as Diffusion of Responsibility or Opacity — the need exists but nobody captures it as a signal anyone can act on.

Signal → Institution

Fails as Bureaucratic Overload or Exclusion — the signal exists but can't reach the body that could act on it.

Institution → Decision

Fails as Coordination Failure or Regulatory Capture — the institution has the signal but can't or won't decide.

Decision → Resources

Fails as Short-Termism or Preventable Scarcity — the decision is made but never funded.

Resources → Implementation

Fails as Maintenance Neglect or Perverse Incentives — funded, but built or run badly.

Implementation → Outcome

Fails as Cascading Infrastructure Failure or Ecological Overshoot — it runs, but breaks under real conditions.

Outcome → Feedback

Fails as Institutional Amnesia or Metric Gaming — the outcome happens but the lesson never loops back.

Data, Privacy & Integrity

What this file does

  • Saves your quest progress, notes, bookmarks, class-finder result, simulator settings, and self-assessments to this browser's IndexedDB, with an automatic localStorage fallback if IndexedDB is unavailable.
  • Never makes a network request. There is no analytics, no telemetry, no fingerprinting, and no hidden call to any server — you can verify this yourself by reading the script, or by checking your browser's network tab while using the file.
  • Never asks for or stores an email address, a name tied to an identity, a password, a payment detail, or any secret or key.
  • Keeps everything on this device unless you explicitly export it.

Your controls

The Export, Import, and Reset controls in the Quest Engine section act on this file's entire saved state — not just quests. Export produces a single, versioned JSON file (schemaVersion included) you can back up, move to another device, or inspect in a text editor. Reset clears everything this file has saved, immediately and without a server-side copy to recover from — export first if you're unsure.

Integrity check (SHA-256)

Paste text — for example, the contents of an exported state file — to compute its SHA-256 hash using your browser's built-in cryptography. Comparing two hashes tells you whether two pieces of text are byte-for-byte identical. It does not tell you who created the text, whether it's trustworthy, or whether this HTML file itself has been modified — no tool inside a file can prove that file hasn't been altered.

Glossary

Plain language first, technical framing second.

Adaptive capacity

The ability to adjust and keep functioning as conditions change. Technically: a system property enabling response to novel disturbance without redesign from scratch.

Bottleneck

The one scarcest thing limiting everything else. Technically: the binding constraint in a system, per weakest-link dynamics.

Cascading failure

One failure quickly causing others. Technically: failure propagation through tightly coupled dependencies.

Commons

A resource shared by a group, owned by no one alone. Technically: a common-pool resource, studied extensively by Elinor Ostrom.

Complex adaptive system

A system made of many interacting parts that adapts as a whole, without central control. Technically: emergent behavior arising from local interactions and feedback.

Externality

A cost or benefit pushed onto someone who had no say in the decision. Technically: an effect on a third party not reflected in the price or terms of a transaction.

Feedback loop

An effect that loops back to influence its own cause. Technically: reinforcing loops amplify change; balancing loops dampen it.

Fragility

Breaking under stress a more resilient system would absorb. Technically: high sensitivity to disorder or shock, the opposite of redundancy and slack.

Goodhart's law

Once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Technically: optimization pressure on a proxy erodes its correlation with the real goal.

Graceful degradation

Failing partially and visibly instead of totally and silently. Technically: a system property that preserves core function and buys response time under partial failure.

Institutional capacity

Whether an organization can actually do what it exists to do. Technically: the combination of authority, resources, skill, and legitimacy needed for effective action.

Interoperability

Independently built things that can still work together. Technically: shared standards or protocols enabling exchange across system boundaries.

Leverage point

A place where a small change produces a disproportionately large effect. Technically: a concept popularized by Donella Meadows describing points of high system sensitivity.

Lock-in

An early choice that becomes hard to escape later. Technically: path dependence reinforced by switching costs or network effects.

Modularity

Parts that can be changed or replaced independently. Technically: low coupling between components, enabling isolated repair or upgrade.

Polycentric governance

Many overlapping centers of authority instead of one. Technically: a governance structure Ostrom found often outperforms both pure centralization and pure fragmentation for commons management.

Public good

Something everyone can use without reducing what's left for others. Technically: non-excludable and non-rival in economic terms — clean air, street lighting, basic research.

Rebound effect

Efficiency gains partly eaten by increased use. Technically: behavioral or economic responses that offset some or all of an efficiency improvement's expected benefit.

Redundancy

A backup so one failure doesn't take down the whole system. Technically: deliberate duplication of critical function to increase fault tolerance.

Regenerative capacity

The ability to actively rebuild capacity, not just avoid losing it. Technically: a system's rate of renewal relative to its rate of use or degradation.

Resilience

The ability to absorb a shock and keep functioning. Technically: capacity to persist, adapt, or transform in response to disturbance.

Second-order effect

The effect of the effect — what a fix causes once it's in place. Technically: downstream consequences distinct from a change's immediate, intended effect.

Slack

Deliberately unused capacity kept in reserve. Technically: buffer capacity that trades short-term efficiency for long-term resilience.

Subsidiarity

Deciding at the smallest scale actually capable of deciding well. Technically: a governance principle allocating authority to the lowest competent level.

System boundary

Where you draw the line around "what counts" in your analysis. Technically: an analytical choice that determines which effects appear as internal versus externalized.

Threshold

A point past which a system shifts into a different, often hard-to-reverse state. Technically: a critical value marking a regime shift or phase transition.
